What a Spread Costs You

Four rule changes, thirteen years apart. You are the one placing the orders, and every screen ends with a number you paid.

1997. Quotes move in eighths of a dollar

A specialist posts a price to buy from you and a price to sell to you. By rule the smallest gap between them is one eighth, 12.5 cents. He is showing size, because that spread pays him to.

You did nothing and you are down money. You bought at his ask and sold at his bid, and the stock never moved. That gap is not a fee anyone announced. It is the price of demanding immediacy, and it is the oldest revenue line in the securities business.

Twelve times cheaper, and that is real. For anyone trading a hundred shares, the modern market is not slightly better, it is dramatically better. Most of the argument about market structure ends here, and for most investors it should.

Now try it with size

He only shows 300 shares at a penny, because a penny does not pay him to show more. Drag the order up and watch what you have to climb through to get filled.

Find the crossover yourself. Somewhere around 3,750 shares the thin penny book becomes the more expensive place to trade, and the old wide-spread market with real size behind it wins. Decimalization did not make trading cheaper. It made small trading much cheaper and large trading harder, which is why institutions started slicing orders into hundreds of small pieces, and why there is now an industry built on doing that for them.

Run ten without co-location, then ten with it. The spread on the screen says one cent either way. What you actually pay does not, because in the time your order spends travelling, the people who can see it coming have already moved. Co-location is sold by the exchange, by the rack, by the metre of cable.
This is the part that is genuinely contested. One reading is that you are being front run. The other is that a market maker who cannot pull a stale quote has to widen it for everyone, and you would pay that instead. Both are defensible and the evidence is mixed. Decide which you believe and be ready to say why.

May 6, 2010. What a thin book does under stress

Nothing here is new. Same penny spread, same 300 shares a level, same market makers who are free to stop quoting whenever they like. A fund now has to sell 20,000 shares and does not care what it gets.

You predicted this two screens ago. Three hundred shares a level and no obligation to quote is a market that works beautifully for a hundred shares and has nothing underneath it when somebody needs to move twenty thousand. The 2010 crash was not caused by a new kind of trader. It was the ordinary structure, asked a question it was never built to answer.
Now tick the band and sell again. The band does not add a single buyer and it does not calm anybody down. It stops a price from printing more than a set distance from where the stock just was, and pauses trading instead. That is the whole fix, and it is why a bigger volatility spike in August 2024 produced nothing like 2010.
